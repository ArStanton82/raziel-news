A bill entering the floor calendar is not a law. That is the difference between politics and procedure, and this week Washington showed it twice.

## What the text provides
H.R. 8957, the "American Reserve Modernization Act of 2026", introduced on 21 May by Republican Representative Nick Begich, sets up a Strategic Bitcoin Reserve and a separate Digital Asset Stockpile within 180 days of taking effect, moving into them the bitcoin and other qualifying digital assets already owned by the federal government, seizures included. The political point is the constraint: the text requires every bitcoin deposited in the reserve to be held "for no less than 20 years" from the date it takes effect, with annual reporting duties to Congress, physical and cyber security measures, and a study on budget-neutral acquisitions. There is also a section reaffirming self-custody as a right.

On 16 September the Financial Services Committee approved it 28 to 21, along party lines. On 24 November it formally sent it to the floor: it sits on Union Calendar number 789, with report H. Rept. 119-894.

## The calendar is the real opponent
The reserve, though, already exists as policy: it comes out of the March 2025 executive order, which funds it with bitcoin the government already holds. A law would make it stable — that is, take it away from the next occupant of the White House. And that is where the time runs short: the 119th Congress adjourns in the coming weeks and there is no room left for a floor vote, Senate scrutiny and a signature. The Block reads it the same way, giving the measure low odds of becoming law. A calendar is a promise of debate, not an outcome.

## Meanwhile, the rules are made by order and by letter
On 25 November Senator Van Hollen and the Democrats' ranking member on the Banking Committee, Elizabeth Warren, together with Tina Smith and Jack Reed, sent two letters to SEC chair Paul Atkins. The first asks him to withdraw the proposed "Regulation Crypto Assets", which would let eligible crypto projects raise capital without registration — up to $5 million over four years, or $75 million over twelve months. The second asks him to revoke the September order exempting Tokenized Securities Venues from registration duties, warning of a "shadow stock market": an equity market outside the ordinary protections. The letters land the day after OKXICE, the joint venture between OKX and ICE, opened as a venue for continuous trading of tokenized US shares.

The picture holds together: when the law doesn't pass, orders, exemptions and senators' letters draw the perimeter. It is a way of legislating that works for as long as the administration signing it lasts. For builders, instability isn't the absence of rules: it is their provisional shape.
