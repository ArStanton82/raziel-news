Three acts, three jurisdictions, one single movement. In the same week Madrid gave its anti-money-laundering unit the power to freeze crypto accounts without a judge, Tokyo questioned access to DeFi itself, and Frankfurt picked an ECB president who distrusts stablecoins. None of these moves bans anything. All of them shift the checkpoint: from the transaction to the access, from possession to infrastructure.

## Madrid: five days without a judge
Royal decree-law 44/2026, issued on 24 November and published the day after, is titled "consumer credit contracts and protection of borrowers in the assignment of doubtful debts". It runs to 178 pages about interest rates and indebted households. At final provision six, however, it amends anti-money-laundering law 10/2010 and gives the Servicio Ejecutivo — Sepblac — the power to suspend pending transactions for up to ten working days and bank, payment or crypto-asset accounts for five, without prior judicial authorisation. Beyond those limits, ratification or extension by the public prosecutor before a judge is required. The measures enter into force on 25 January 2027, Congress must ratify or repeal the decree within thirty days, and anyone who fails to execute the order faces penalties. The editorial point isn't only the power: it's where it travels. A crypto measure inside a consumer-credit decree gets approved without public debate ever seeing it.

## Tokyo: the question before the rule
On 26 November the Financial Services Agency published a discussion paper on DeFi and non-custodial wallets. It isn't a rule: it's a questionnaire for the digital finance study group, which has been debating it since October and aims at an interim summary for early 2027. Inside are the numbers that explain the hurry — 86.6 billion dollars of DeFi TVL in June, up more than 85% on 2023 — and the uncomfortable questions: what extra money-laundering risk a protocol that anyone can reach without identification poses, and what protection users who hold their own keys deserve. Cointelegraph reads into it the hypothesis of discouraging access to DeFi services without adequate controls. Japan, in short, is deciding whether to regulate the protocol or the way you get to it.

## Frankfurt: continuity that talks about stablecoins
On 26 November the European Council proposed Pablo Hernández de Cos for the ECB presidency: former governor of the Bank of Spain, today general manager of the Bank for International Settlements, he would take over from Christine Lagarde, leaving on 7 February 2027, on a non-renewable eight-year mandate. Alongside him Laurence Boone and Markus Brunnermeier were named to the executive board, replacing Isabel Schnabel and Philip Lane. The procedure isn't closed: consultation of the European Parliament and of the Governing Council, and the formal decision, are still missing. On substance, Cointelegraph recalls that De Cos has questioned the practicality of stablecoins for mass-use payments and backs the development of the digital euro.

Three different approaches — an emergency decree, a consultation paper, a technical appointment — converge on one idea: the risk isn't in the transaction, it's at the front door. Anyone who wants to understand where regulation is heading over the next two years would do better to follow the final provisions of omnibus decrees than the press releases.
