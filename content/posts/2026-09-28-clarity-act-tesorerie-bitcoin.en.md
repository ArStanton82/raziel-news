While Washington shelves yet another attempt to give the crypto market a rulebook, the market goes it alone: it buys Bitcoin, it accumulates, it builds. That is the most interesting contrast of these hours.

## The CLARITY Act: sunk, but not dead
The Digital Asset Market Clarity Act did not pass. The Senate rejected the cloture motion on 15 September: 60 votes were needed, about 50 arrived. Here verification demands precision, because sources diverge: according to CNBC the tally was 50 to 49, according to Galaxy and the Bitcoin Foundation 49 to 50. The difference is not idle — it concerns the exact number of votes against, including Republican senator Thom Tillis, who would have switched his vote for procedural reasons. What is certain is the wider picture: every Democrat against, Lummis blaming politics for the defeat — "Democrats care more about hating President Trump than about making good policy" — and a text that remains technically reintroducible thanks to the motion to reconsider signed by Tillis. CoinDesk's analysis is retrospective, not a new story: the fact itself is two weeks old.

## The treasuries: the part that does not wait
On the other side, accumulation does not stop. Capital B, a listed French company, bought another 13 Bitcoin for about 970,000 euros, taking its total to 3,538 BTC. Michael Saylor, for his part, revived his coded message — "Even more orange" — which historically precedes a purchase announcement; and Strive CEO Matt Cole also hinted at new buys. These are signals, not confirmations: the official communication normally arrives the next day.

## The technological backdrop
Meanwhile Vitalik Buterin published his essay on the "cryptographic world computer": Ethereum, he writes, is no longer merely a blockchain but a hybrid architecture combining chains and modern cryptography, with an eye on the Hegotá fork and on tools such as recursive STARKs and formal verification. It is a vision, but a well-argued one, widely picked up by the specialist press.

## Conclusion
Regulation limps, adoption runs. Whether that is good news or a risk depends on which of the two tracks we treat as the train and which as the siding. For now, the market chooses not to wait.
