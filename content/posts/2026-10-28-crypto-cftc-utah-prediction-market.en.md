On 26 October the Commodity Futures Trading Commission filed a complaint against the State of Utah and its attorney general, Derek Brown, before the District Court of Utah. The stated aim is to stop state laws from shutting down markets registered with the CFTC. It is the second half of a match played on an old boundary: where federal oversight of derivatives ends and where a state's right to call something gambling begins.

## Utah's week

The context is a case already open. A few days earlier, Brown's office had sued KalshiEX and affiliated companies in state court, arguing that the platform offers bets on sports, elections and politics in violation of Utah law, and asking for the return of profits it considers unlawful: up to 60 million dollars, according to Cointelegraph's reconstruction.

Utah's legal premise is solid at the local level: the state constitution forbids the legislature from authorising gambling, and Utah is one of only two states where the ban is constitutional. Nor is it an isolated case: in February Kalshi had itself sued Utah in federal court, arguing that the Commodity Exchange Act prevented the state from regulating the platform. In August Judge Robert Shelby sided with Utah, and on 8 September the Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals refused Kalshi a stay on the application of state law pending appeal.

## The federal argument

The CFTC answers on jurisdiction, not on the merits. In the official release, chairman Michael Selig argues that Utah is trying to shut down prediction markets "across a wide variety of markets" and points out that event contracts do not only serve hedging and investment: they produce information about the outcome of future events. Congress, he adds, gave the Commission "exclusive responsibility" for overseeing those markets.

The agency's language is deliberately constitutional: limiting the operation of registered exchanges "obstructs Congress's decision to federally preempt" state law. And it is not a one-off: the CFTC says it has opened proceedings against nine other states and filed briefs in four federal appeals courts and one state supreme court.

## Why it matters for crypto too

Kalshi is not a crypto platform, but the ground is the same one Polymarket and the venues that settle accounts in stablecoins move on: event contracts, financial surveillance, boundaries between authorities. Kalshi's spokesperson, Jacki McGavick, summed up the industry's position: a national derivatives market overseen by the CFTC answers to federal law, and no single state should hold a veto.

The real subject of the dispute is not technology. It is whether a federally registered exchange can be shut down by a state court, and the answer may only come from the Supreme Court, where New Jersey has already asked to intervene in September on a similar case, while comparable suits remain pending in Michigan, Ohio, Kentucky, Nevada, Massachusetts, Wisconsin and New York.
