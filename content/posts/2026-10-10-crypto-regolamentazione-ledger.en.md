There is a difference between regulating a technology and integrating it. For years crypto regulation was mostly a matter of permissions and prohibitions; this week the tone changes. Three different jurisdictions took steps that are no longer about whether crypto exists, but about how to live alongside it: listed products, compliance deadlines, technical rules for operators.

## Bangkok opens the door to ETFs

The Thai SEC has finalised the rules that let local managers launch crypto ETFs, in force from 16 October. Initially only bitcoin and ether will be admitted, with a passive strategy and an average net exposure of at least 80% to the single asset per accounting year. The funds will have to be listed exclusively on the Stock Exchange of Thailand, use regulated custodians and will not be allowed to lend on margin. 16 October is a regulatory starting date, not a launch date: each manager will still have to obtain approval for its own product.

## Brussels tightens the screws on stablecoins

On the European front, ESMA has published an opinion giving providers authorised under MiCA a maximum of three months to wind down services on non-compliant stablecoins — asset-referenced and e-money tokens. Platforms must block new access and prevent clients from increasing their exposure; national authorities will supervise residual positions, to be closed by 3 January 2027. The implicit target is unauthorised tokens such as USDT and PYUSD: compliance stops being a matter for issuers and becomes a requirement for platforms.

## Washington modernises and seizes

In the United States, the SEC has proposed amendments to the "cross-trading rule" of the Investment Company Act, which under certain conditions allows securities trades between a registered fund and its affiliates: the amendments would modernise and extend it, cutting trading costs to the benefit of investors. On the sanctions front, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced that the US expects to seize roughly one billion dollars in crypto linked to Iran within the week, as part of the "campaign of absolute isolation". It is an announced intention, however, not a completed seizure, and it is unclear whether it overlaps with the roughly 900 million already claimed in May.

## The flip side: the supply chain

The counterpoint comes from hardware. Ledger has asked the South-East Asian reseller CryptoBilis to suspend sales and shipments, while it investigates losses estimated by on-chain investigators at more than 86 million dollars across Ethereum, TRON and Bitcoin. The company has not confirmed the figure nor established whether the devices were tampered with; CZ spoke of a possible localised attack on the supply chain. Finally, El Salvador: the reserve has passed 7,800 BTC, but for the IMF the recent additions are private donations, not public funds.

## Conclusion

The thread linking Thailand, Brussels, Washington and a tampered metal box is the same one: crypto is becoming big enough to deserve infrastructure, and infrastructure brings rules, custodians and responsibilities with it. The novelty is not that regulators are dealing with it, but that they are doing so with technical instruments: the ambivalent sign of a sector that has entered adulthood.
