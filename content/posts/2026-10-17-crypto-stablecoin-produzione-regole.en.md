For years stablecoins were sold as promising infrastructure. This week the vocabulary changed: people talk about production, onboarded customers, deadlines. The shift from future to present tense, though, is happening while the regulatory frame is still under construction.

## From pilot to production
On 15 October Visa announced that Visa Direct's stablecoin capabilities are moving from pilot to commercial deployment. Inyo Global is the first enabler: businesses can pre-fund cross-border payments in USDC in near real time across more than 180 countries, without depending on traditional banking windows. The same day Circle released its first-half 2026 numbers: over 36 trillion dollars in onchain volume, 315 billion USDC minted and redeemed, roughly 77% of global stablecoin transaction volume in June. The flip side sits in costs: 819 million dollars, 59% of revenue, went to distribution partners and transaction costs. Distribution is the business.

On the retail side, Charles Schwab completed its Schwab Crypto rollout on 15 October, opening Bitcoin and Ether trading to all eligible clients of a brokerage worth 13.33 trillion dollars; Solana, Avalanche and Chainlink are due by the end of the year. New York, Louisiana and the US territories are excluded, and assets custodied through Paxos carry no SIPC or FDIC coverage. Deutsche Bank is a separate case: press coverage spoke of Bitcoin custody "for its 28 million clients", but the bank's own statement is explicit — the service is aimed at institutional and corporate clients in Europe, not retail. The caution in the headline is part of the story.

## The rule arrives after the market
On 8 October ESMA published an opinion asking MiCA-authorised providers to wind down services tied to non-compliant stablecoins, with remaining exposures closed within three months, that is by 8 January 2027. On 12 October the Financial Stability Board handed the G20 its note "Global Stablecoin Arrangements": regulatory frameworks not yet tested under severe stress. On 5 October the CFTC opened an advance notice of proposed rulemaking on crypto-asset transactions and markets (Regulation CTX and Regulation CAM), published in the Federal Register on 13 October, with comments due by 14 December. The rule, in short, arrives while the market is already in production.

## A risk with an uncertain date
On 15 October nine crypto companies launched the Quantum Alliance. The alliance estimates that 6.4 million bitcoin, 30.2% of supply, already have exposed public keys and are therefore theoretically vulnerable to a future quantum computer — which does not exist today, and no current machine can exploit the flaw. Members do not agree on the date: some call 2028 realistic, others consider it unlikely. There is no agreement either on freezing vulnerable funds, a decision that would touch the neutrality of the network. The disagreement, more than the threat, is the news.

The trajectory is clear: financial infrastructure has already absorbed stablecoins, while authorities are still writing the rules and the industry debates risks that may not materialise for a decade. The gap between these three speeds is where the next phase will be played out.
