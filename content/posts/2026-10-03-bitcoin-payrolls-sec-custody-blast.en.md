2 October was one of those days when Bitcoin's price tells the macroeconomy and everything else tells the story of adoption. The two stories run on separate tracks and are worth keeping apart, because the first is short-term noise and the second, slowly, is the substance.

## The number that moved the market

September's American jobs report showed just 29,000 new non-farm jobs, against roughly 90,000 expected, with the unemployment rate rising to 4.2% from 4.1%. Revisions also cut the earlier months, taking August from 162,000 down to 133,000. Bitcoin, already climbing in the hours before, broke past $87,000 and touched 87,229, then fell back towards 85,300, giving up about $2,000 of those gains. Treasury yields reversed direction. The dominant reading is that a struggling jobs market pushes a rate hike further away; but the fact that the move was immediately rejected by a wall of sell orders is a reminder that price is not a verdict, it is a negotiation.

## Rules and banks, where the change is slow and real

On the structural side, the day delivered three pieces. On 1 October the SEC proposed a custody framework for advisers and regulated funds: in limited circumstances — when no suitable custodian exists — the adviser may custody clients' assets directly, and state trust companies are recognised as custodians. Public comment stays open for sixty days. The next day, Absa became the first African bank to offer digital asset custody to institutional clients in South Africa, with Bitcoin as the main asset, Ripple technology and initial support for BTC, ETH, XRP Ledger and USDC. And US spot ETFs opened October with $102.7 million in net inflows, after a third quarter worth $6.34 billion. None of this moves the price the way a jobs figure does, but this is where Bitcoin enters regulated balance sheets.

## Blast and the bill for infrastructure

To remind us that expansion is not automatic, the opposite case arrives. Blast announced the shutdown of its Ethereum layer-2: value locked has collapsed by 98% from a peak of more than $2 billion in June 2024, down to around $32 million, and the cost of running the network has outstripped revenue — $1,793 collected last month against a peak of roughly $3.5 million. Users have until 26 October to withdraw through the interface. The message is brutal and useful: a blockchain does not live on one-off incentives, but on demand that stays.

A market jolting on jobs data, rules advancing at a snail's pace, and infrastructure closing when the numbers don't add up. The underlying question stays the same: how much of Bitcoin's value depends on the American credit cycle and how much, instead, on the patient work of those who make it usable inside the financial system.
