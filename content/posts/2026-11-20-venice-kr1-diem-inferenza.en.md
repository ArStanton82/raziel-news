KR1 plc, an onchain infrastructure company listed on the London Stock Exchange, announced on 19 November that it had started «DIEM infrastructure operations». The mechanics are simple: KR1 holds 557 DIEM, bought separately from its VVV positions; every staked DIEM guarantees one dollar of credit a day for running models on Venice. They keep those credits switched on and resell them. At list prices the capacity is worth 557 dollars a day: roughly 50 thousand dollars across the ninety days covered by the announcement.

## A credit that does not keep

Venice's documentation is clear on one point, and that point changes everything: the daily allocation resets at 00:00 UTC, and unspent credit does not accumulate. A DIEM is not a meal voucher: it is an access right that dies every night.

From this a trade is born. Whoever does not consume their whole allocation loses it; whoever has customers willing to pay in USDC has a reason to buy it at a discount. KR1 is not betting on the token going up: it is making a market in a perishable commodity, with a warehouse that empties at midnight.

## The numbers, and what they are

Over the ninety days to 16 November, according to the statement, KR1 processed roughly 1.92 million requests from third-party customers through Surplus Intelligence, serving 101.5 billion tokens. The proceeds arrive in USDC. October's earnings imply an estimated annualised yield of 5.45% on the fair value of the DIEM employed at the end of the month.

That 5.45% is the company's own estimate, over a single month, bound — in KR1's words — to vary with the supply of and demand for inference: not a guaranteed return, not a network metric. But the language counts: a listed company that describes inference as infrastructure and publishes its yield is saying that compute has stopped being a bet.

## The margin is in the expiry

There is a second, subtler effect. The credit is fixed in dollars: one DIEM always returns one dollar a day, whatever happens to token prices. If the cost of inference keeps falling — and that is the trajectory of the whole sector — the same dollar buys more tokens every month, and the position becomes relatively more useful over time. It is a way to be exposed to AI without betting on which model will win.

What remains to be seen is how deep this market is. Five hundred and fifty-seven DIEM are a drop: annual capacity stops at around 203 thousand dollars. But KR1 is not alone: it also supplies DIEM to Diem Relay, which pools suppliers' tokens and resells the resulting capacity, and Venice's Mint Rate — the amount of sVVV needed to mint a DIEM — rises with supply. If the model holds, demand for DIEM grows together with demand for compute.

The story is not the 5.45%. It is the shape: tokenised, perishable capacity, traded on the spot. An infrastructure that looks more and more like an energy market and less and less like a token to keep in a drawer.
